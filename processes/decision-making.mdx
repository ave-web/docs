Every decision at the company has a Directly Responsible Individual (DRI) — one person who is accountable for making the call and ensuring it gets done. The DRI model is how we move fast without losing clarity, and how we avoid the slowdown and diffused accountability that comes from decision-by-committee.

## The DRI Model

A DRI is the single person responsible for a decision. They gather the input they need, weigh the options, make the call, and own the outcome. Assigning a DRI doesn't mean everyone else is excluded — it means the decision has a clear home. When there's no DRI, decisions stall, get revisited endlessly, or simply don't get made.

The DRI should be the person closest to the work and most qualified to make the call. In practice, that's usually the person leading the project, the team, or the relevant function — not necessarily the most senior person in the room.

<Note>
  Having a DRI doesn't mean others aren't consulted — it means one person is accountable for the outcome.
</Note>

## Decision Types

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Type 1: Irreversible" icon="bolt">
    High-stakes decisions that are difficult or impossible to undo — like major product pivots, significant hires, or large financial commitments. Escalate appropriately, consult widely, document your rationale, and take your time. Getting these wrong is costly.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Type 2: Reversible" icon="rotate">
    Low-stakes decisions that are easy to course-correct if they turn out to be wrong. Make these quickly and move on. Don't apply the same rigor you'd use for a Type 1 decision — over-processing reversible decisions is its own form of waste.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Cross-Team" icon="users">
    Decisions that meaningfully affect more than one team. The DRI facilitates the process and makes the final call, but stakeholders from each affected team must have a genuine opportunity to provide input before the decision is made.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Individual" icon="person">
    Decisions that fall entirely within your own scope and don't materially affect others. You are the DRI. Use your judgment, act with ownership, and document the decision if it might be relevant for others later.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## The DACI Framework

For decisions that involve multiple people, use the DACI framework to clarify who does what. Assign each role before the decision process begins to avoid confusion about who has final say.

| Role | Who They Are | Responsibility |
|------|-------------|----------------|
| **Driver** | The DRI | Leads the process, gathers input, makes the final call, and ensures the decision is executed. |
| **Approver** | Senior stakeholder or exec | Must sign off before the decision is finalised. Only required for high-stakes or cross-functional decisions. |
| **Contributor** | Subject-matter experts and affected parties | Provide input, data, and perspective to inform the decision. They advise but don't decide. |
| **Informed** | Anyone who needs to know the outcome | Notified after the decision is made. Not part of the decision process itself. |

## Documenting Decisions

<Steps>
  <Step title="State the decision clearly">
    Write down the decision in plain language in a shared doc or Slack thread. Be specific — vague decision records create ambiguity later.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Capture the options and your reasoning">
    Document the alternatives you considered and explain why you chose this path. Future you — and future teammates — will want to understand the thinking, not just the outcome.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Note who was consulted">
    Record who provided input and who was the Approver, if applicable. This creates accountability and shows the decision was made with appropriate consultation.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Link to or create a Company Brain page">
    If the decision sets a precedent, changes a process, or affects how the team operates, link it to the relevant Company Brain page or create a new one. Decisions that live only in Slack will be forgotten.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Escalation

You don't need to escalate most decisions — that's the point of the DRI model. But some calls genuinely need to go up the chain. Escalate when a decision:

- **Sets a company-wide precedent** that will be hard to walk back or that others will look to as a reference point.
- **Affects more than two teams** in a material way, and you haven't been able to reach alignment through the DACI process.
- **Has irreversible consequences** — significant financial, legal, reputational, or strategic implications that exceed your authority.

When you escalate, bring a **recommendation**, not just a problem. Come with the options you considered, the tradeoffs you see, and a clear point of view on what you think should happen. Escalating a decision without a recommendation asks someone else to do the thinking you should have done yourself.

<Tip>
  Speed is a feature. Make reversible decisions fast. Save careful deliberation for the calls that can't be undone.
</Tip>
