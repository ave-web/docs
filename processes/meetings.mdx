Meetings are expensive. Every hour on the calendar costs at least two people's focused time, and often far more. We treat them as a last resort for things that truly require real-time discussion, and we run them with care when we do. A well-run meeting drives things forward; a poorly run one drains energy and leaves everyone wondering why they were there.

## When to Schedule a Meeting

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Meet">
    Schedule a meeting when the situation genuinely benefits from real-time discussion:

    - **High-stakes decisions with real ambiguity** — when the right answer isn't obvious and different perspectives need to collide live.
    - **Team alignment on direction** — when everyone needs to land on the same page at the same moment, not in a Slack thread that trails off.
    - **Sensitive interpersonal topics** — performance conversations, conflicts, and difficult feedback should happen face to face, not over text.
    - **Live brainstorming** — when building on each other's ideas in real time will produce better output than async contributions.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Don't Meet">
    Default to async when a meeting isn't actually necessary:

    - **Status updates** — use written async updates in Slack or your project management tool. No one needs to sit in a room to hear what you finished this week.
    - **Questions with a clear answer** — ask in Slack. You'll get an answer faster and won't pull someone out of deep work.
    - **Approvals** — route through email or a shared doc. An approval doesn't need a calendar invite.
    - **Announcements** — post in the appropriate `#announce-` channel. Synchronous time is too valuable for one-way information sharing.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Before the Meeting

- Write and share an agenda at least 24 hours before the meeting. If you can't write one, reconsider whether the meeting needs to happen.
- Identify a **facilitator** (keeps things on track) and a **note-taker** (captures decisions and actions) before the meeting starts.
- Only invite people who genuinely need to be there. Every extra attendee is a cost — protect their time.
- Define a clear goal for the meeting: are you making a decision, aligning on direction, or generating ideas? Write it in one sentence at the top of the agenda.

## During the Meeting

- Start on time. Waiting for latecomers penalises people who respected the calendar invite.
- Follow the agenda. If new topics come up, add them to a parking lot and address them afterward.
- Capture decisions and action items in real time in the meeting doc — don't rely on memory or chat logs.
- End with clarity: every action item needs an owner and a due date before the call wraps up.

## After the Meeting

<Steps>
  <Step title="Post the meeting notes">
    Share the notes in the relevant Slack channel or link to the doc from the calendar event so everyone — including those who couldn't attend — can see what was discussed.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Assign action items">
    Make sure every action item has a named owner and a specific due date. Unassigned tasks don't get done.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Update Company Brain">
    If the meeting produced a decision, a new process, or a change in direction, update the relevant Company Brain page — or create a new one. Decisions shouldn't live only in meeting notes.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Cancel meetings that have run their course">
    Review recurring meetings periodically. If a standing meeting no longer has a clear purpose, cancel it. Your team's calendar will thank you.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Meeting Types

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Weekly Team Sync">
    **Cadence:** Weekly, 30–45 minutes
    **Format:** Rotating facilitator; standing agenda covering priorities, blockers, and team announcements.
    **Who runs it:** Team lead or a rotating facilitator from the team.
    This is the team's main touchpoint for staying aligned on work in flight. Keep it focused — it's not a status report, it's a space to surface blockers and coordinate on shared priorities.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="1:1s">
    **Cadence:** Weekly or biweekly, 30 minutes
    **Format:** Employee-driven agenda; manager provides support, feedback, and context.
    **Who runs it:** The direct report owns the agenda; the manager facilitates coaching and unblocking.
    1:1s are for the employee, not the manager. Use them to discuss career growth, raise concerns, and get context you can't get elsewhere. Come with a prepared agenda.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Retrospectives">
    **Cadence:** End of each sprint or project milestone
    **Format:** Structured discussion: what went well, what didn't, what to change.
    **Who runs it:** Team lead or a designated facilitator.
    Retros are only valuable if people speak honestly and the outputs lead to real changes. Capture action items and revisit them at the next retro to see if things actually improved.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Planning Sessions">
    **Cadence:** Start of each sprint, quarter, or planning cycle
    **Format:** Review goals, prioritise work, assign ownership.
    **Who runs it:** Team lead with input from all team members.
    Come to planning sessions prepared. Review the backlog or priorities list before the meeting so you can make decisions quickly rather than reading documents aloud in the room.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="All-Hands">
    **Cadence:** Monthly or quarterly, 60 minutes
    **Format:** Company updates, Q&A, and team spotlights.
    **Who runs it:** Executive team, with contributions from across the company.
    All-Hands are for company-wide context and connection. Submit questions in advance using the linked form. Recordings and notes are posted in `#announce-all-hands` after each session.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

<Tip>
  A meeting without an agenda is a waste of everyone's time. If you can't write a one-line goal for the meeting, don't schedule it.
</Tip>
